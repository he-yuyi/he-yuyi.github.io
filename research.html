Is Greenium a Reflection of Core Inflation Risk?

with Zhenyu Gao, Chanik Jo, Yoshio Nozawa

Climate finance · Inflation · Asset pricing

Green assets carry greater core inflation risk. Accounting for that exposure reduces the measured greenium.

Best Paper Award · Korean Finance Association Autumn Conference, 2024

Request draft ↗
Abstract

We present a novel stylized fact that green stocks carry higher inflation risk compared to brown stocks, performing poorly at times of unexpected inflation. Given this fact, can the outperformance of green stocks over brown stocks (the "greenium") be explained as compensation for inflation risk? We find that the magnitude of the greenium decreases by 31% and 54% for Scope 1 and Scope 2, and the greenium becomes statistically insignificant after controlling for individual stocks' core inflation risk exposure. These findings are robust to excluding brown industries and are not driven by the post-COVID inflationary period. Overall, our results suggest that the greenium is in part a reflection of inflation risk compensation.

Key results
  • Controlling for individual stocks’ core inflation risk exposure reduces the equity greenium by 31% for Scope 1 and 54% for Scope 2.
  • The greenium becomes statistically insignificant after the adjustment. The findings are robust to excluding brown industries and are not driven by the post-COVID inflationary period.

02Working paper

Who Bears the Climate Risk? State Guarantees and the Pricing of Physical Hazard in the Municipal Bond Market

with Changhyun Ahn, Chanik Jo

Municipal bonds · Physical risk · State guarantees

State guarantees shift most of the pricing of local physical hazard away from municipal borrowers.

Request draft ↗
Abstract

Twenty-two states guarantee school district bonds by statute, replacing district credit with state credit. We ask who pays for physical disaster risk. Across 716,211 uninsured general obligation maturities offered between 2009 and 2022, a standard deviation increase in county hazard raises an unguaranteed issuer's offering yield by 4.58 basis points. By contrast, the same increase changes a guaranteed issuer's yield by less than two basis points. Against unguaranteed school districts alone, the same increase still costs a guaranteed district about one basis point. A statute therefore shifts the bulk of the price of county hazard off the borrower.

Key results
  • Across 716,211 uninsured general obligation maturities offered from 2009 to 2022, a one-standard-deviation increase in county hazard raises unguaranteed offering yields by 4.58 basis points.
  • The corresponding change for guaranteed issuers is less than two basis points. A comparison limited to school districts leaves a premium of about one basis point.

03Working paper

Beyond Size and Value in China: A Stochastic Discount Factor from Sixty Characteristics

with Kiryoung Lee, Chanik Jo, Gang Li

Machine learning · Chinese equities · Asset pricing

Liquidity and volatility characteristics drive the out-of-sample performance of machine-learning discount factors for Chinese A shares.

Request draft ↗
Abstract

We compare four factor models and 12 machine-learning models for Chinese A shares out of sample from January 2015 to June 2026. Every machine-learning discount factor built on 60 characteristics attains a Sharpe ratio between 1.42 and 3.00, and every factor model between 0.38 and 0.94. The characteristics a model uses therefore decide more than the estimator that combines them. Net of each stock's transaction cost, a discount factor estimated by adversarial networks earns the highest Sharpe ratio, 1.49 against 1.25 for one estimated by shrinkage and 0.14 for the four-factor China model. Liquidity and volatility characteristics, not size and value, produce that performance, and the Sharpe ratio rises as the universe widens to smaller, less liquid stocks, consistent with mispricing that short-sale constraints and arbitrage costs keep in place. Factor models for A shares should therefore include the liquidity and volatility characteristics that the three- and four-factor models omit.

Key results
  • Out of sample from January 2015 to June 2026, the 12 machine-learning models attain Sharpe ratios of 1.42–3.00, compared with 0.38–0.94 for the four factor models.
  • After stock-specific transaction costs, the adversarial-network SDF earns a Sharpe ratio of 1.49, compared with 1.25 for shrinkage and 0.14 for the four-factor China model.
  • Liquidity and volatility characteristics drive performance. Sharpe ratios rise as the universe expands to smaller, less liquid stocks, consistent with mispricing and limits to arbitrage.
